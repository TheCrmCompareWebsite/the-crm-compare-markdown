## Introduction

PropertyBase Salesforce Edition is a real estate CRM delivered as a package that runs within Salesforce. Each customer receives a Salesforce system tailored to property sales, with contacts, listings, and deals sharing a single database and Salesforce's reporting and security. Lone Wolf Technologies sells it to brokerages, multi-office groups, and franchise networks in blocks of at least ten users and does not market it to individual agents.

The product began as a Salesforce real estate application from a Munich start-up and was first listed on Salesforce's app marketplace in 2008. The company behind it was incorporated in 2010. It later moved its base to the United States, joined the Boston Logic group in 2017, and was acquired in August 2021 by Lone Wolf Technologies. Founded in Canada in 1989, Lone Wolf is now headquartered in Dallas and backed by private equity. Matt Fischer has served as Lone Wolf's chief executive since February 2026. The Propertybase name now applies only to this Salesforce product, alongside two additional CRMs Lone Wolf sells under its own brand.

Its pitch is a single shared real estate system for an entire organization, built on Salesforce rather than on the vendor's own software. A broker can give every office and agent the same system, let managers oversee their people, and reshape it the way any Salesforce customer adapts its own, which is also why it is made for organizations with the budget and staff to run it.

## Fit

Propertybase Salesforce Edition is intended for real estate offices and franchises.

A broker licenses one system that the whole office can share. Agents keep their clients private, while the broker sees everything. New inquiries are automatically assigned to agents, and listings, offers, and closings are stored in the same records as clients, so the broker can track every deal in the office. The ten-license minimum that rules out individual agents is roughly the size of a small office, so it is no obstacle here.

A franchise or multi-office group gets the most from the platform's scale. Separate offices and territories can run on a single system under their own managers. Listings can be sent to property portals worldwide, and the CRM integrates with the rest of Lone Wolf's brokerage software. Because the system is built on Salesforce, a franchise with its own technical staff can tailor it to individual needs.

A high-volume team is a suitable fit. It provides the lead handling it needs, with new inquiries routed to the right member, follow-up started automatically, and each member's work visible to the team leader. It falls short of ideal because it lacks built-in texting, click-to-dial, or call capture, which a team working fresh leads relies on every day. A team smaller than ten still pays for ten seats.

All of these buyers will notice the same defects. The system cannot be tested before buying, the commitment runs year to year, setup and training cost extra, help comes through tickets rather than a phone line, and customers describe problems that remain unfixed. The larger question is the product's future, since its owner shows far more public development on its two additional CRMs, which seem to compete with PropertyBase.

## Features

**Score: 6.2/10**

The product’s strongest capabilities support a brokerage's lead and deal flow. Leads come in from portals, Facebook ads, and web forms; the system checks for duplicates, routes them to agents by source, rotation, or availability, and starts follow-up plans automatically. It stores buyer requirements and matches them with listings, while showings, offers, and closings each have their own records, allowing a deal to be tracked from the first inquiry through the closing checklist. Built on Salesforce, the system also offers strong permissions: managers can see their agents' work, while agents see only their own. Individual users can also be restricted from exporting data, deleting records, or viewing commission fields.

The gaps fall on the agent's side of the desk. There is no built-in texting, no click-to-dial, and no call capture. The content library is thin, with a handful of email templates, one drip sequence, and no newsletters, eCards, or Spanish versions. MLS search and saved-search alerts work only for customers who also buy a Propertybase website.

## Terms of Service

**Score: 2.5/10**

There is no free trial or monthly plan; prospective customers book a demonstration and sign a one-year agreement. The [Salesforce Edition Addendum](https://get.lwolf.com/rs/803-SUI-561/images/Propertybase-Salelsforce-Edition-Addendum.pdf?version=0) sets each subscription and renewal at one year. It provides that it "shall automatically renew unless terminated by either Party by providing thirty (30) days’ prior written notice." Under the [Master Subscription Agreement](https://get.lwolf.com/rs/803-SUI-561/images/Propertybase-Master-Subscription-Agreement.pdf?version=0), fees are paid in advance for the entire term, and "All fees are non-refundable and non-cancellable." A brokerage that misses the notice date is committed to another full year, and one that leaves early still owes the fees for the remainder of the term.

Prices may rise at renewal with 30 days' notice, or at any time if a third-party provider, such as Salesforce, increases its fees. This has already happened: in June 2026, [Software Finder](https://softwarefinder.com/crm/propertybase-salesforce-edition) and [Luxury Presence](https://www.luxurypresence.com/?p=44432) both listed the product at $79 per user; the vendor's page now lists it at $89.

Reviewers describe billing practices that exceeded what their contracts allowed. Customers on [G2](https://www.g2.com/products/propertybase/reviews/propertybase-review-7482997) and [Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/122129/Propertybase/reviews/) report unexpected renewals, charges above their contract price, invoices that continued after they backed out, and an inability to remove their card from billing. These reviews span 2020 to 2023, but together suggest the company has not always honored its own terms.

The obligations run mostly one way. A customer who ends the contract early, for any reason other than an uncured breach by Lone Wolf, continues paying all fees through the end of the term "as liquidated damages and not as a penalty." Lone Wolf can end the contract only for cause, and disputes are governed by Massachusetts law. Lone Wolf may also end the contract immediately if a competitor of Lone Wolf comes to control the customer. The Salesforce Edition Addendum provides that if Salesforce suspends or ends access to the shared Salesforce system, including when the Salesforce agreement it relies on expires, the customer "will remain liable to Company for all fees owed," with no refund.

### Privacy Policy

**Score: 9.5/10**

Propertybase's contract treats a brokerage's client data as the brokerage's confidential property, and Lone Wolf says it handles that data only on the customer's behalf.

The [Master Subscription Agreement](https://get.lwolf.com/rs/803-SUI-561/images/Propertybase-Master-Subscription-Agreement.pdf?version=0) states that the customer "retains all rights to and ownership of Customer Data." Lone Wolf may use that data only while the subscription is active and solely to provide, support, secure, improve, and develop the service. The agreement also adds that "in no case shall Company sell any Customer Data." Customer Data is also defined as the customer's confidential information, which Lone Wolf must not disclose to anyone outside the staff and advisers who need it to do their jobs.

Lone Wolf's [privacy policy](https://www.lwolf.com/privacy-policy) describes the same arrangement from the consumer's perspective. Information that agents collect about their clients is processed by Lone Wolf "as a processor on behalf of our customer," and a client who wants to see or delete that information is referred back to the agent or brokerage that holds it. In plain terms, the brokerage controls its clients' data, and Lone Wolf acts as its contractor.

Lone Wolf also sells other CRMs and brokerage software. The Propertybase agreement gives Lone Wolf no right to share a brokerage's client records with those products, and its confidentiality terms prohibit disclosure beyond the staff who need the data to run the service.

The weak point is the limited window. After the contract ends, a customer has only five days to request a copy of its data in writing, and Lone Wolf then has 30 days to provide it. A brokerage leaving Propertybase should export everything before the contract ends rather than rely on that window.

The documents disagree on what happens to client data afterward. Section 4.3 of the Master Subscription Agreement states that once the export window has passed, Lone Wolf "may delete" the data under its then-current deletion policies, which is a permission rather than a promise. Section 6 of the [Salesforce Edition Addendum](https://get.lwolf.com/rs/803-SUI-561/images/Propertybase-Salelsforce-Edition-Addendum.pdf?version=0) states that customer data "will be deleted by SFDC in accordance with the SFDC documentation," naming Salesforce as the party and pointing to documents outside the agreement, with no timetable. Lone Wolf should state in one place who deletes a former customer's data and by when.

>
> - +0.5: The agreement states that customer data is confidential, will not be disclosed, and will never be sold (three of the four protections)
> - +1: Lone Wolf states that it handles client information only as a processor for its customers
> - +1: Customers can obtain a copy of their data after the contract ends
>

## Conclusion

Propertybase Salesforce Edition is a real estate-specific CRM built for brokerage offices and franchise groups that want every agent working in one controlled system. A large team that works its leads hard can also make it serve. The ten-license minimum excludes individual agents and small teams, and even the buyers it is built for should weigh the risks before committing.

Its strengths are organizational: it follows a deal from first inquiry to closing, controls who can see what across offices and teams, and keeps a brokerage's client data in the brokerage's hands under its contract. Its weaknesses lie in the software rather than in the software itself: the contract terms are strict, the help that matters most costs extra, and customers describe problems that remained unfixed, and sales promises the product did not keep.

Two red flags stand out. The product shows little public sign of development, while its owner is focusing its efforts on two other CRMs. That owner has also replaced most of its senior leadership this year, making the product's future harder to read. The customer evidence is also old, with no reviews on any site in the past two years, so buyers have no recent outside account of how the product performs today.

At more than $10,000 per year for the smallest purchase, it is poor value for anyone who will not fill all ten seats. For a brokerage or franchise that will, the cost per agent is reasonable, and the platform earns a place on a shortlist, provided a product roadmap, support response times, and a plan for exporting the data are agreed in writing before signing.
